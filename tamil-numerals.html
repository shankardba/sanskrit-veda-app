Tamil / Numerals

Tamil Illakkam (இலக்கம்) — the traditional numeral system — has mostly been replaced by Arabic numerals in everyday use, but it survives on temple inscriptions, old milestones, and in the number words themselves, which are still how Tamil is actually spoken. It shares nothing with Devanagari's numeral glyphs (see Sanskrit Numerals) beyond the same underlying base-10 value — the two scripts count the same way but write it completely differently.

Zero's modern word, சுழியம் (suḻiyam, "spiral"), describes the shape of the glyph itself. Old Tamil used a different word for zero, அன்று ("not-day," i.e. nothing) — number words shifted over time the same way the glyphs did.

Ten, hundred, thousand aren't built from the digits above

Devanagari numerals (and Arabic ones) are purely positional — 10 is written by reusing the glyphs for 1 and 0 in two columns. Tamil Illakkam isn't: ten, hundred, and thousand each get their own dedicated, unrelated character, the way Roman numerals hand X and C their own symbols instead of composing them from I. Everything above one thousand is then built by repeating these three:

Ten thousand is written ௰௲ (ten beside thousand, "ten of a thousand"), a hundred thousand ௱௲, a million ௲௲ — stacking the three symbols rather than adding more digit columns the way 10,000 does in Arabic numerals.

Nine is the odd one out

தொ + [unvoiced form of the next word's first consonant]
தொண்ணூறு — thoṇṇūṟu, 90
(தொ + unvoiced ண் from நூறு)

Nine's prefix isn't fixed the way 1–8's are — it's built fresh each time from whatever base-10 word follows, by voicing-devoicing sandhi at the seam (the same kind of junction Vedic Sanskrit's own sandhi does, see Sanskrit Grammar, just triggered by which number is next rather than by a vowel or visarga).

Fractions had their own dedicated symbols too

Traditional Tamil accounting used a whole family of symbols for common fractions, down to units as small as 1⁄320 — added to Unicode only in 2019. The everyday ones are still recognisable:

கால்
kāl — ¼
அரை
arai — ½
முக்கால்
mukkāl — ¾

The full historical system (muntiri, kāṇi, mā, vīsam, and on down toward fractions of a single grain of rice) is out of scope here — a genuinely deep rabbit hole of pre-decimal Tamil land and currency accounting, not something a general reader needs to recognise letters.

Large-number names keep going well past thousand — இலட்சம் (ilaṭcam, lakh, 10⁵) and கோடி (kōṭi, crore, 10⁷) are the two still in everyday use, matching the same lakh/crore grouping Indian English uses for Sanskrit- and Hindi-derived numbers. Ordinals ("second," "third"...) are just the cardinal number plus -ஆம் (-ām): இரண்டு → இரண்டாம் (second). See Grammar for how words like these actually inflect in a sentence.
