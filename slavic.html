Sanskrit / Sanskrit ↔ Slavic

Cousins, not parent and child

Sanskrit was written down about two thousand years before the first Slavic text, so it's easy to read it as the ancestor. The vowels show it can't be. Proto-Indo-European, the shared ancestor, had three vowels e, o and a. Sanskrit merged all three into a. Slavic kept e and o apart, correctly, word after word. A daughter language can't un-merge vowels its parent had already collapsed.

asti↔jest "is": PIE *h₁ésti had e
nakta↔noć "night": PIE *nókʷts had o

Sanskrit has a in both. Slavic knows which one was which.

Russian, Serbian, Croatian …

"Cyrillic languages" isn't quite the right grouping. Cyrillic is a script, adapted from Greek letters in 9th-century Bulgaria. Croatian is written in Latin letters, Serbian in both, and Mongolian uses Cyrillic without being Slavic at all. The family this page compares is Slavic, whatever alphabet each language uses.

Which Slavic language is closest to Sanskrit?

By descent, every Slavic language is exactly as related to Sanskrit as every other: they all split from one ancestor. What differs is how much of the shared inheritance each has kept. Serbian / Croatian comes out on top

It keeps the most inherited grammar that Sanskrit also has (a living vocative, 7 cases, the aorist, strict second-position clitics), and it independently re-grew all three look-alikes: -m on every verb, r as a vowel, and a pitch accent. That double effect is why it can feel the most Sanskrit-like. Russian, the best-known Cyrillic language, keeps the least.

One branch over: Lithuanian

sūnuḥ ↔ sūnus · vṛkaḥ ↔ vilkas · devaḥ ↔ dievas
For sheer resemblance, the closest living language to Sanskrit isn't Slavic. It's Lithuanian, Slavic's Baltic sibling, which kept the noun endings Slavic wore away. It's written in Latin letters, which is one more reason "Cyrillic" is the wrong filter.

Two roots for "to be", in both

as- : asti ↔ jest  ·  bhū- : bhavati ↔ byti

Sanskrit builds "to be" from two PIE roots: as- for "is" and bhū- for "become / was". Slavic does exactly the same: jest / je from as-, and byti / biti / быть from bhū-. The present tense lines up ending for ending, down to the "they are" form, where santi and Old Church Slavonic sǫtъ keep the same nasal.

The dual, still alive in Slovene

svaḥ · sthaḥ · staḥ ↔ sva · sta · sta

Sanskrit has a full grammatical number for pairs. So did Old Church Slavonic, and Slovene still does: midva sva "we two are", vidva sta "you two are". Set beside Sanskrit's dual of as- ("we two are", "you two are", "they two are"), the forms are nearly letter for letter.

Elsewhere only relics survive. Russian два стола "two tables" keeps an old dual ending, and Serbian/Croatian plural -ama / -ima most likely comes from the old dual ending -ma.

The s-aorist, with RUKI on both sides

a-jai-ṣ-am "I conquered" ↔ reko-h "I said"

Sanskrit forms one past tense by adding -s- to the root. In ajaiṣam that s has become ṣ after i. Slavic inherited the same -s- aorist, and RUKI turned it into x after the same sounds. That is where the final -h of Serbian rekoh and Bulgarian казах comes from. One difference: Sanskrit also puts an "augment" a- in front (a-jaiṣam), and Slavic never had it.

"Make someone wake": causatives

bodhayati ↔ buditi  ·  vāhayati ↔ voziti

PIE made causative verbs by switching the root vowel to o and adding -éye-. Sanskrit gives bodhayati "wakes someone", from budh- "be awake", the root of Buddha, "the awakened one". Slavic gives buditi / будить, the everyday verb for waking someone up. "Transport" works the same way: Sanskrit vāhayati, Slavic voziti. The o is disguised in Sanskrit but plain in Slavic, which is the vowel test again.

Inherited

Participles in -ta and -na

mṛta ↔ mrtav "dead"  ·  pūrṇa ↔ plný "full"

Sanskrit makes past participles with -ta (kṛta "done") or -na (bhinna "split"). Slavic uses the same two suffixes for its passive participles: Russian убит "killed" (-t), написан "written" (-n). Some of these hardened into ordinary adjectives on both sides. "Dead" and "full" were PIE participles, *mr̥tós and *pl̥h₁nós.

Little words in second place

vājaś ca me  ↔  Brat mi je dao knjigu

Vedic Sanskrit tucks unstressed pronouns in right after the first word of a clause. This site's own Chamakam is built on one: vājaś ca me, "and for me, strength", with the enclitic me in second place, over and over. The rule is called Wackernagel's Law, after the linguist who described it for Sanskrit and Greek.

Serbian/Croatian follows it more strictly than any other living Indo-European language. In Brat mi je dao knjigu ("my brother gave me the book"), mi is the same enclitic as Sanskrit me, in the same second slot. Czech and Slovene keep the rule. Russian mostly lost it.

The smallest words

na ↔ ne "not"  ·  pra-pitāmaha ↔ pra-djed "great-grandfather"

Negation runs the vowel test in two letters: PIE *ne became Sanskrit na and Slavic ne. The prefix pra-, "one generation further back", survives unchanged on both sides: Sanskrit prapitāmaha, Serbian pradjed, Russian прадед.

Rebuilt independently

Every verb ending in -m

jānāmi ↔ znam "I know"  ·  but Russian знаю

In PIE only a small class of verbs took -mi for "I": Sanskrit asmi "I am", dadāmi "I give". Sanskrit spread it to almost every verb (bhavāmi, jānāmi). Serbian/Croatian and Slovene made the same move on their own, spreading -m from jesam and dam to znam "I know" and pišem "I write". Russian didn't: знаю, пишу. It's the same ending and the same spread, but as two separate events.

Near miss

bh where Slavic has m

sūnubhiḥ ↔ synъmi "with sons"

Some endings fill the same slot with a different consonant. Sanskrit's instrumental plural -bhiḥ and dual -bhyām use bh. Slavic, Baltic and Germanic use m: Old Church Slavonic synъmi, Lithuanian sūnumis. Same position, different sound. That fits cousins, not a parent and child.

Slavic only

Where Slavic went its own way: aspect

pisati / napisati · писать / написать "write / get it written"

Nearly every Slavic verb comes as a pair: imperfective for an action in progress, perfective for a finished one, usually made with a prefix. It's the backbone of Slavic grammar, and Sanskrit has nothing like it. Sanskrit marks this kind of difference through its tense stems instead. Aspect is a Slavic invention, not something that came through Sanskrit.

Borrowed, not inherited

Part of the Indo-Iranian feel of Slavic is real, but it arrived by contact. Iranian-speaking steppe peoples (Scythians, Sarmatians), whose languages were close relatives of Vedic Sanskrit, lived next to early Slavs and left words behind. These are Sanskrit's cousins' words, not Sanskrit's.
