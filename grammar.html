Sanskrit Language / Grammar

Sanskrit grammar (vyākaraṇa, one of the six traditional Vedāṅga disciplines) was studied and codified from the later Vedic period onward, culminating in Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī ("eight chapters"), around the 4th century BCE — a complete, algebraic, rule-based description of the language so compact it can be recited in about two hours. Kātyāyana and Patañjali added commentary a few centuries later (together the three are called the Trimuni Vyākaraṇa, "grammar of the three sages"), and the tradition continued through Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita's 17th-century Siddhānta Kaumudī into 19th-century European Sanskrit scholarship. This page covers Classical Sanskrit — the language Pāṇini standardized. The older Vedic language behind the chants on this site differs in several specific ways; see Vedic Grammar for those, rather than repeating this page there.

Sandhi

Sanskrit words change shape at their edges depending on what they touch — the same mechanism already seen shaping numerals (ṣaṣ → ṣaḍviṃśati) and the alphabet's own place/manner grid on the Characters page. Two flavors:

External sandhi

between two separate words
Similar vowels merge into one long vowel (a + a → ā); dissimilar ones glide or diphthongize (i + a → ya, a + i → e). Voiced consonants pull a following or preceding voiceless one into voicing to match — regressive assimilation, for ease of pronunciation across the word boundary.

Internal sandhi

inside a single word, at a root/suffix seam
More tightly constrained by the root's own structure — the same place-of-articulation and voicing/aspiration logic, but applied where a suffix meets a root rather than where two words meet.

Vedic external sandhi is noticeably more variable/less fixed than the standardized Classical rules — one of several differences covered on the Vedic Grammar page.

Vowel gradation — guṇa & vṛddhi

Inherited from Proto-Indo-European ablaut (the same mechanism behind English sing/sang/sung/song), this is the single most load-bearing pattern in Sanskrit word-formation: a root's vowel systematically strengthens or weakens depending on what's being built from it. Three grades, keyed to the same five vowel-places already catalogued on the Characters and Numerals pages:

 ai / īu / ūṛḷ
Zero grade —i / īu / ūṛḷ
Guṇa (1st) aeoaral
Vṛddhi (2nd) āaiauārāl

As a rule of thumb: an accented root syllable takes guṇa or vṛddhi; an unaccented one reduces to zero grade. भू (bhū, "become") shows all three: bhū- (zero) → bho- (guṇa, seen in bhavati, "he becomes") → bhāu--type vṛddhi forms in further derivatives. This one alternation underlies a huge share of both verb-stem formation and noun/adjective derivation.

Sanskrit's pitch accent (udātta/anudātta/svarita — the same marks already rendered on this site's chant pages and catalogued on the Characters page) governed which grade a root took. It was still alive in Pāṇini's time but had disappeared from popular speech within a few centuries of the Classical standard being set — see Vedic Grammar.

Nominal declension

Every noun and adjective is declined across three axes — 3 numbers (singular, dual, plural) × 3 genders (masculine, feminine, neuter) × 8 cases — with adjectives simply taking on whatever gender/number/case the noun they describe has. The 8 cases each mark a distinct grammatical role:

Verb conjugation

A finite verb form is the product of five independent axes — 3 persons × 3 numbers × 3 voices (active/middle/passive) × 4 moods (indicative/optative/imperative/conditional) × 6 tenses (present/imperfect/perfect/aorist/future/periphrastic future) — the same "dimensional grid" logic already seen driving the numeral and declension systems, just with more axes.

Beyond the finite forms above: five "secondary conjugations" (passive, intensive, desiderative, causative, denominative) each rebuild a new stem from the root with its own sense, and three non-finite forms exist — participles, a single infinitive, and the gerund.

Compounds (samāsa)

Fusing multiple words into one is a defining Sanskrit habit, inherited from Proto-Indo-European but expanded far beyond it — later literary Sanskrit builds compounds of a dozen or more members. Four relationships cover almost every compound:

Dvandva — "and"

coordinative: X and Y
rāma-lakṣmaṇau — "Rama and Lakshmana"

Tatpuruṣa — "of/for/in X"

determinative: first member modifies second by an implied case relation
rāja-puruṣaḥ — "the king's man" (genitive)
A descriptive subtype, karmadhāraya, fuses an adjective straight onto its noun: nīla-utpalam → nīlotpalam, "blue lotus."

Bahuvrīhi — "having X"

possessive: the whole compound describes something outside itself
pīta-ambaraḥ — "yellow-garmented", i.e. Kṛṣṇa (not the garment itself)

Avyayībhāva — "in the manner of X"

adverbial: the whole compound becomes an indeclinable
yathā-śakti — "according to (one's) ability"

Indeclinables & syntax

Prepositions, adverbs, particles, conjunctions, and interjections never decline — no case, number, or gender to track, which is part of why they're easy to underestimate: a handful of particles (sma, a-/an-) quietly carry grammatical weight, like turning a present-tense verb into a past one.

Because case endings — not word order — carry the grammatical relationships, Sanskrit word order is largely free; Pāṇini deliberately left syntax unfixed in the Aṣṭādhyāyī. In practice there's a strong default tendency toward subject–object–verb.

Sanskrit's vocabulary and construction options run famously deep: Sanskritist Daniel Ingalls found roughly fifteen distinct ways to say "you must fetch the horse" — mixing active/passive, imperative/optative, an auxiliary verb, or any of three gerundive forms, each giving a different metrical shape.
