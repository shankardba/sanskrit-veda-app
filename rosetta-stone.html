Sanskrit and Tamil, side by side — the same sounds in two scripts with no common ancestor.

Sanskrit ↔ Tamil / Rosetta Stone

Sanskrit's Devanagari and Tamil's own script are unrelated in origin (see Tamil Characters) — this page isn't about either script on its own, it's the two laid side by side, sound by sound. Most of it lines up cleanly. Where it doesn't, that gap is usually the most interesting part: a sound one language marks that the other doesn't, or a distinction one keeps that the other collapses. Every correspondence here is already verified elsewhere on this site — Sanskrit in Tamil Script against real chant text — this page just puts it in one glance-able table.

Eight of Tamil's twelve vowels map onto Devanagari one letter for one letter, no adjustment needed either direction.

Tamil keeps a distinction Sanskrit doesn't have

எ/ஏ (short/long e) — Sanskrit ஏ has only ए
ஒ/ஓ (short/long o) — Sanskrit ஓ has only ओ

Sanskrit keeps four vowels Tamil doesn't have

ऋ ṛ · ॠ ṝ · ऌ ḷ · ॡ ḹ
syllabic r/l — no Tamil letter is ever a vowel nucleus by itself

Sanskrit's ए and ओ are always long — there's no short-e/short-o phoneme to distinguish them from, so one letter each is enough. Tamil's short e/o are genuinely separate phonemes from long ē/ō, so it needs both எ and ஏ, both ஒ and ஓ. On this one point, Tamil's vowel inventory is finer-grained than Sanskrit's — worth noting since the reverse (Sanskrit distinguishing more than Tamil) is true almost everywhere else on this page.

Sanskrit's Vedic-only ळ and Tamil's ழ்/ள் aren't the same sound and don't share a common origin, but it's a genuine curiosity that both languages independently ended up with "extra" retroflex consonants beyond what their own core systems predict — some linguists attribute Sanskrit's retroflex series in general to prehistoric contact with Dravidian languages, though the specifics are still debated, and nothing here should be read as claiming ळ and ழ்/ள் are historically the same letter.
