Characters

Sanskrit's Devanagari and Tamil's own script are unrelated in origin (see Tamil Characters) — this page isn't about either script on its own, it's the two laid side by side, sound by sound. Most of it lines up cleanly. Where it doesn't, that gap is usually the most interesting part: a sound one language marks that the other doesn't, or a distinction one keeps that the other collapses. Every correspondence here is already verified elsewhere on this site — Sanskrit in Tamil Script against real chant text — this page just puts it in one glance-able table.

Tamil keeps a distinction Sanskrit doesn't have

எ/ஏ (short/long e) — Sanskrit ஏ has only ए
ஒ/ஓ (short/long o) — Sanskrit ஓ has only ओ

Sanskrit keeps four vowels Tamil doesn't have

ऋ ṛ · ॠ ṝ · ऌ ḷ · ॡ ḹ
syllabic r/l — no Tamil letter is ever a vowel nucleus by itself

Sanskrit's ए and ओ are always long — there's no short-e/short-o phoneme to distinguish them from, so one letter each is enough. Tamil's short e/o are genuinely separate phonemes from long ē/ō, so it needs both எ and ஏ, both ஒ and ஓ. On this one point, Tamil's vowel inventory is finer-grained than Sanskrit's — worth noting since the reverse (Sanskrit distinguishing more than Tamil) is true almost everywhere else on this page.

Grammar & Syntax

Sanskrit (Indo-European) and Tamil (Dravidian) belong to two unrelated language families — nothing here claims a common ancestor for their grammars any more than for their scripts. But two languages that have shared a subcontinent for three thousand years pick up real structural resemblances along the way, on top of a few that look like coincidence and a few genuine, principled differences. The full mechanics of each side are already covered on Sanskrit Grammar and Tamil Grammar — this section is just the two held up against each other.

Where the two genuinely converge

Same default word order, same reason

Subject – Object – Verb
Both default to SOV rather than English's SVO. In both, it's a default rather than a rule — because case endings, not position, mark who did what to whom, word order is free to move for emphasis or meter in both languages. That freedom is exactly what lets Vedic chant lines rearrange for the metrical count without becoming ungrammatical.

No verb "to be" in the present

rāmaḥ sundaraḥ  /  kumār māṇavaṉ
"Rama [is] handsome," "Kumar [is] a student" — both languages simply juxtapose subject and predicate in the present tense and let the sentence stand without a copula. English can't do this; Sanskrit and Tamil both can, for an unrelated pair of grammars to independently land on.

Case does what English prepositions do

8 Sanskrit cases ↔ 9 Tamil cases
Nominative, accusative, instrumental, dative, ablative, genitive, locative, and vocative line up almost one-for-one between the two systems (see Sanskrit Grammar and Tamil Grammar for the full tables) — Tamil's one extra, the sociative ("along with"), is a case Sanskrit folds into the instrumental instead of splitting out.

Sandhi happens at every seam, in both

sandhi (संधि) ↔ puṇarcci (புணர்ச்சி)
Both languages smooth the join wherever two sounds meet — a word boundary in Sanskrit's external sandhi, a suffix boundary in Tamil's agglutination — by merging vowels, inserting a glide, or assimilating a consonant so the result stays pronounceable. Different trigger, same instinct.

One long "word" standing in for a whole clause

nīlotpalam  /  pōkamuṭiyātavarkaḷukkāka
Sanskrit gets there by samāsa — fusing independent word-stems together with no inflection between them (nīla + utpalam → nīlotpalam, "blue lotus"). Tamil gets there by agglutination — gluing one bound suffix after another onto a single root (see Tamil Grammar's eight-morpheme example, "for the sake of those who cannot go"). Opposite mechanisms, same outcome: a single orthographic word doing the work of an English clause.

Relative clauses without a relative pronoun

yaḥ ... saḥ  /  participle + noun
English needs "who"/"which" to embed a relative clause. Sanskrit instead pairs a correlative pronoun across two clauses (yo dharmaṃ carati, sa sukhī bhavati — "who practices dharma, he becomes happy"). Tamil instead folds the whole relative clause into a participle sitting directly before the noun it modifies, with no pronoun at all. Neither language reaches for a word meaning "who" — two different fixes for the same gap English fills with a relative pronoun.

Where they genuinely part ways

Two structural differences matter enough that no amount of surface similarity should paper over them — both already covered in full on each language's own grammar page, restated here only for contrast:

Fusion vs. agglutination

devāya (one ending)  /  -kk(u) + -āka (stacked)
A single Sanskrit case ending fuses case, number, and gender into one unsegmentable piece. Tamil's endings stack, each suffix carrying exactly one piece of meaning and nothing else — see Tamil Grammar.

Gender: grammatical vs. rational/irrational

3 genders  /  5 categories
Sanskrit sorts every noun into masculine/feminine/neuter regardless of what it refers to. Tamil sorts by personhood first — only humans and deities get true masculine/feminine — then crosses that with number, so even a named female animal is grammatically "irrational," not feminine.
