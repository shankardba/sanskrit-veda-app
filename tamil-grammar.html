Tamil / Grammar

Tamil grammar was first systematically described in the Tolkāppiyam (roughly 300 BCE–300 CE), the oldest surviving Tamil text of any kind; modern usage mostly follows the 13th-century Naṉṉūl, which restated and updated its rules. Traditional grammar splits into five parts — eḻuttu (letters, already covered on Characters), sol (words), poruḷ (poetic subject matter), yāppu (prosody), and aṇi (rhetorical figures). This page covers sol — how words themselves are built and inflected — and leaves the three poetics-focused parts aside.

Words are stacked, not just inflected

Tamil is agglutinative: a word is a root with one or more suffixes glued on in sequence, each carrying its own single piece of meaning — unlike Sanskrit, where a single ending fuses case, number, and gender together into one unsegmentable piece (see Sanskrit Grammar). There's no upper limit on how many suffixes can stack, which is how Tamil gets real, ordinary words that translate to whole English clauses:

Nine noun cases — one more than Sanskrit's eight

Cases are just another suffix in the stack (root + optional plural -kaḷ + case ending). The extra one beyond Sanskrit's eight (see Sanskrit Grammar) is the sociative — "along with" — which Sanskrit folds into the instrumental instead of splitting out on its own.

Locative and dative each pick between two suffix families depending on whether the noun is "rational" (people, deities) or "irrational" (everything else) — a distinction with no Sanskrit equivalent, and one that runs through the gender system too, below.

Five genders, not three — rational/irrational crossed with number

Sanskrit's masculine/feminine/neuter (see Sanskrit Grammar) sorts every noun by grammatical gender alone. Tamil's pā (பால்) sorts by rationality first — only humans and deities get a true masculine/feminine split — then crosses that with number, giving five categories rather than three:

Even a named female animal is grammatically irrational (neuter), not feminine — gender agreement tracks personhood, not biological sex.

அழிந்துக்கொண்டிருந்தேன் (aḻintukkoṇṭiruntēṉ) — "I was being destroyed." Person/number/gender always comes last and is built from the same oblique pronoun forms used elsewhere in the sentence: -ēṉ (1st singular), -āy (2nd singular), -āṉ/-āḷ (3rd masc./fem. singular), -ōm (1st plural) — recognisably the same personal markers, reused as a suffix instead of a separate word, the way Sanskrit's verb endings are historically related to its pronouns too but no longer show it synchronically.

Puṇarcci — sandhi at the seams of agglutination

Every suffix boundary above is a potential sandhi site: Tamil's own puṇarcci (புணர்ச்சி) smooths the join the same way Vedic Sanskrit's sandhi does (see Sanskrit Grammar) — inserting a glide, doubling a consonant, or dropping a vowel so the result stays pronounceable.

vakuppu + ilவகுப்புக்குள் (vakuppukkuḷ) — a consonant doubles at the seam
nuḻainta + uṭaṉநுழைந்தவுடன் (nuḻaintavuṭaṉ) — a glide (v) is inserted between the vowels

One pronoun distinction Sanskrit doesn't mark

Tamil's first-person plural splits into inclusive and exclusive "we" — நாம் (nām) if the listener is included, நாங்கள் (nāṅkaḷ) if not — a distinction neither Sanskrit nor English marks at all.

Word order is subject–object–verb, like Sanskrit's own default order, and Tamil is null-subject (a verb alone can be a complete sentence) with no verb "to be" for equational sentences — "Kumar is a student" has no word corresponding to "is." Left out here: the twelve-plus verb-conjugation classes each with their own stem-change pattern, the oblique-stem formation rules nouns undergo before certain suffixes, and poruḷ/ yāppu/aṇi (poetic content, prosody, and rhetoric) — genuine grammar, but about composing literature rather than reading it.
