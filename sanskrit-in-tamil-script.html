Tamil / Sanskrit in Tamil Script

vignanam.org's Tamil-script Chamakam is the same Krishna Yajurveda text as the Chants page's Devanagari version — not a translation, a transliteration, for reciters who read Tamil fluently but not Devanagari. Tamil's own eighteen consonants (see Characters) are smaller than Sanskrit's — no native letters for voiced or aspirated stops, and no palatal sibilant distinctions at all. Making up that gap uses exactly two devices, and which one gets used is entirely predictable from where the gap actually is.

Where Tamil already has a letter: use it directly

Grantha ஜ/ஶ/ஷ/ஸ/ஹ (see Characters) needs no modification — Tamil simply has no native letter anywhere near these sounds, so the borrowed letter stands in on its own.

Where Tamil doesn't: a superscript digit on the plain letter

Tamil's stop letters (க ட த ப) are each already ambiguous between voiceless and voiced in native Tamil (see Characters) — but writing Sanskrit precisely needs all four of a Devanagari row's variants kept distinct. vignanam.org resolves this with superscript ²/³/⁴ on the base letter: ² aspirated voiceless, ³ voiced, ⁴ voiced aspirated — a documented convention (Unicode's own standard even reserves superscript digits for exactly this), not a site-specific invention.

The same pattern carries into the other three stop columns — ட²/ட³/ட⁴ (retroflex), த³/த⁴ (dental), ப³/பி⁴ (labial) — each attested at least once: பஷ்ட²வாட் (paṣṭhavāṭ), த்³யு (dyu, dyumnair), கோ॒³தூ⁴மா (gōdhūmā), வாஜேபி॒⁴ர் (vājebhir).

Accent marks don't care which script they're sitting on

The udātta/anudātta/svarita pitch-accent strokes (॑ ॒ ᳚, already catalogued on the Sanskrit Characters page) are a phonetic layer riding on top of the letters, not part of either script — the exact same combining marks sit over Tamil consonants here as sit over Devanagari ones. அக்³னா॑விஷ்ணூ carries its udātta stroke in the identical place अग्ना॑विष्णू does.

"Cha mē" has three faces, and the two scripts don't always agree on which

The Chamakam's own "cha mē" isn't always spelled the same way — which form shows up is fully predicted by the sound immediately before it, on both scripts, in two of the three cases:

After a vowel
அஶ்மா॑ ச மே
aśmā cha mē — stays separate
After visarga (-aḥ)
த³க்ஷ॑ஶ்ச மே
dakṣaśca mē — fuses to -śca
After anusvara (-aṃ)
ப³லம்॑ ச ம॒ / बल-ञ्च म॒
Tamil: balaṃ cha ma (separate) — Devanagari: bala-ñca ma (fused)

The third row is a genuine disagreement between the two source pages, not a rendering choice on this site's part: vignanam.org's Devanagari page fuses -m + cha into -ñca (the regular nasal-assimilation sandhi), but its Tamil page for the identical line keeps them separate, ப³லம் ச rather than a fused equivalent. Both are legitimate ways to notate the same spoken sandhi — Tamil orthography is generally more conservative about writing consonant fusions than Devanagari is (see Characters: conjuncts are rare in native Tamil to begin with) — but it means the Tamil and Devanagari pages of the same chant aren't always byte-for-byte parallel underneath the transliteration.
