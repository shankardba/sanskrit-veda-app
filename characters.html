Not full vowels, but signs riding on one: anusvāra nasalises the vowel into a following consonant's place; chandrabindu is a lighter, more open nasalisation (as in हँस, "swan") — this site transliterates both as ṃ, same as the chant pages; visarga is a light voiceless breath after the vowel; avagraha marks a dropped अ in sandhi.

Two very rare Vedic-only marks — jihvāmūlīya and upadhmānīya (breath before क/ख and प/फ) — are left out for now since they don't occur anywhere in this site's chants, and the fonts in use here don't render them cleanly. They can be added later if a chant that uses them is.

Building a syllable — mātrā signs

Every consonant already carries an inherent /a/ (क is ka, not bare k) — swap that /a/ for another vowel by adding a mātrā sign, and the result is one glyph for the whole syllable, the same "consonant + vowel = one shape" idea covered on the Tamil Characters page for its own script. Below, every consonant on this page run through the ten vowels most syllables actually use, plus ऋ/ṛ (common enough in ordinary words — kṛṣṇa, vṛkṣa — to earn its own column here; ॠ/ऌ/ॡ combine the identical way but are rare enough in practice to leave out of the grid).

Complex characters — conjunct consonants (saṃyuktākṣara)

When one consonant is immediately followed by another with no vowel between them, Devanagari fuses the two into a single glyph rather than writing the first with a visible virama (्) and leaving it stranded next to the second — this is the single biggest structural difference from Tamil, whose own Characters page notes that Tamil "almost never fuses two consonants into a single conjunct glyph" the way this page's whole script does routinely. Every conjunct below is drawn from words that actually occur in this site's own chants, not invented for the occasion.
