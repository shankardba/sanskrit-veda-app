Reading the Lineages

What the tree shows, and the story of each branch.

Tree Guide

What is a paramparā?

Paramparā (परम्परा) means "one after another": a chain of teachers, each passing on what they received from their own teacher. Indian traditions value a teaching partly by its unbroken line, and monks still recite their lineage before they teach. The tree on the previous page follows six such chains and the places where they touch.

How to read the tree

  • Solid gold: teacher to disciple (initiation or direct discipleship).
  • Dotted gold: membership of a monastic order. The rounded "Purī", "Giri" and "Sarasvatī" boxes are the orders themselves.
  • Grey dashes: founding or leading an institution (dashed boxes), which is how most lines reach the present day.
  • Pink dots: a documented meeting outside the formal line.
  • Teal: a teaching passed on through texts or translation rather than in person.
  • Blue dash-dot: an influence of ideas that scholars debate, not a teacher. Square boxes with a teal edge are texts and schools rather than people.
  • Purple dashes: a link that rests on tradition or a single source and isn't historically attested.
  • Red dots: a separation or dispute.
  • Crimson dash-dot: a parallel between branches. These appear only when you select someone, so the tree stays readable.

Lineages before Śaṅkara

Divine teachers → the sages → Gauḍapāda → Govinda → Ādi Śaṅkara

Ādi Śaṅkara (c. 700–750 CE) stands where three kinds of inheritance meet: a chain of teachers that tradition recites, a body of texts he wrote commentaries on, and ideas from neighbouring schools that scholars still debate.

1 · The chain of teachers (guru-paramparā)

Advaita monks still chant this verse before teaching:

नारायणं पद्मभुवं वसिष्ठं शक्तिं च तत्पुत्रपराशरं च ।
व्यासं शुकं गौडपदं महान्तं गोविन्दयोगीन्द्रमथास्य शिष्यम् ॥
श्रीशङ्कराचार्यमथास्य पद्मपादं च हस्तामलकं च शिष्यम् ।
तं त्रोटकं वार्त्तिककारमन्यानस्मद्गुरून् सन्ततमानतोऽस्मि ॥
nārāyaṇaṃ padmabhuvaṃ vasiṣṭhaṃ śaktiṃ ca tatputra-parāśaraṃ ca | vyāsaṃ śukaṃ gauḍapadaṃ mahāntaṃ govinda-yogīndram athāsya śiṣyam || śrī-śaṅkarācāryam athāsya padmapādaṃ ca hastāmalakaṃ ca śiṣyam | taṃ troṭakaṃ vārttikakāram anyān asmad-gurūn santatam ānato 'smi ||
To Nārāyaṇa, to lotus-born Brahmā, to Vasiṣṭha, to Śakti and his son Parāśara; to Vyāsa, Śuka, the great Gauḍapāda, Govinda lord of yogis, and then his disciple Śrī Śaṅkarācārya; and to Śaṅkara's disciples Padmapāda, Hastāmalaka, Troṭaka and the author of the Vārttikas (Sureśvara), and to all our other teachers: I bow, again and again.

The verse continues past Śaṅkara to his four disciples. Tradition places one at the head of each of his four maṭhas, and from those maṭhas come the ten Daśanāmī orders ("ten names"). Three of those orders carry branches on this tree: Purī, Giri and Sarasvatī.

2 · The texts he inherited

The prasthāna-traya ("three foundations"): the Upaniṣads, especially Uddālaka Āruṇi's tat tvam asi ("you are that") and Yājñavalkya's neti neti ("not this, not this"); Bādarāyaṇa's Brahma Sūtras, whose author tradition identifies with Vyāsa; and the Bhagavad Gītā. Every Vedānta school must comment on all three, and Śaṅkara's commentaries on them are his main works. Behind him also stand earlier Vedāntins now known only from quotations. He cites "the revered Upavarṣa" and Dramiḍācārya, and argues against Bhartṛprapañca. Closest of all is Gauḍapāda's Māṇḍūkya Kārikā, the earliest surviving systematic Advaita text, which Śaṅkara commented on.

3 · Influences scholars debate

Mahāyāna Buddhism. The Kārikā's fourth chapter borrows heavily from Madhyamaka and Yogācāra: no-origination (ajāti) echoes Nāgārjuna. Rival schools later called Śaṅkara a "Buddhist in disguise" (pracchanna bauddha). Most modern scholars see Gauḍapāda adapting Buddhist arguments and Śaṅkara rooting them back in the Upaniṣads.

Mīmāṃsā. From the ritual-exegesis school of Śabara and Kumārila he took rules for reading scripture, but not its view that the Veda is mainly about ritual action. Legend has him defeat the Mīmāṃsaka Maṇḍana Miśra in debate, after which Maṇḍana became his disciple Sureśvara.

Bhartṛhari, the grammarian-philosopher of "Brahman as the Word" (śabda-brahman), is also sometimes named as an influence, but how much is disputed.

Dates

Modern scholarship mostly places Śaṅkara around 700–750 CE (older books say 788–820). Some maṭha traditions give much earlier dates, back to the 5th century BCE.

The four maṭhas

Śaṅkara → four disciples → four seats → ten orders

Tradition says Śaṅkara founded a monastic seat (maṭha or pīṭha) at each of the four corners of India and put one of his four disciples at the head of each. Each seat guards one Veda and one of the Upaniṣads' four "great sayings" (mahāvākya), and each ordains monks into particular orders among the ten names (Daśanāmī). Their heads are still called Śaṅkarācārya.

What this means for the tree: the orders in bold have branches here. The Ramakrishna line (Purī) and the Sivananda–Chinmaya line (Sarasvatī) both trace to Śṛṅgerī, and Yukteswar and Yogananda's Giri vows trace to Jyotirmaṭha.

How certain is this? The four-seat scheme is first described in the later hagiographies of Śaṅkara (the Śaṅkara-digvijaya texts), and accounts differ on which disciple headed which seat. Śṛṅgerī's continuous line is the best documented. Jyotirmaṭha lay vacant for well over a century before its revival in 1941. The Kanchi Kāmakoṭi Pīṭham, home of Mahā Periyavā, claims to be a fifth seat founded by Śaṅkara himself, a claim the other four dispute.

Ramakrishna–Vivekananda

Purī order → Totāpurī → Ramakrishna → Vivekananda → Ramakrishna Order → Sarvapriyananda

Ramakrishna took sannyāsa from Totāpurī, a Purī monk. Vivekananda brought the teaching to the West in 1893 and founded the Ramakrishna Mission and the Vedanta Society of New York. That society is now led by Swami Sarvapriyananda.

Kriyā Yoga

Babaji → Lahiri Mahasaya → Sri Yukteswar → Yogananda → SRF · Ananda

The Kriyā technique came from Mahavatar Babaji through Lahiri Mahasaya to Sri Yukteswar, who also took formal vows in the Giri order. Yogananda brought Kriyā to America in 1920. After him the line divides: Self-Realization Fellowship, led today by Swami Chidananda, and Swami Kriyananda's Ananda, which separated from it.

Chinmaya

Sarasvatī order → Sivananda + Tapovan Maharaj → Chinmayananda → Chinmaya Mission

Swami Chinmayananda took sannyāsa from Swami Sivananda and studied Vedānta under Tapovan Maharaj in the Himalayas. From 1951 he taught the Gītā and Upaniṣads in English to anyone who came, and Chinmaya Mission grew around those talks.

The Ramana branch

Arunachala → Ramana → many lines, no single successor

Ramana took no vows and had no human guru: he said the hill Arunachala was his guru. He joins the root by teaching rather than ordination, having translated Śaṅkara's works into Tamil. He also named no successor, so his influence spread along several separate lines.

The texts

Śivaprakāśam Pillai's questions of 1902 became Nān Yār? ("Who am I?"). Gaṇapati Muni recorded the Sanskrit Ramaṇa Gītā. Muruganār's Guru Vācaka Kōvai was revised by Ramana himself, and the Tamil line passes from Muruganār through Sādhu Ōm to the translator Michael James.

Those who realised in his presence

Lakṣmaṇa Swāmy (1949), and through him Mathru Śrī Sāradā (1978). Papaji (met 1944) became the most prolific teacher. Besides Gangaji and Mooji, his Lucknow students who went on to teach include Eli Jaxon-Bear, Isaac Shapiro and Madhukar. Another, Andrew Cohen, later broke with him publicly.

Carrying him to the world

Paul Brunton's book (1934) first made him known in the West, after the Kanchi Śaṅkarācārya sent Brunton to him. Somerset Maugham visited in 1938, and Ramana is widely taken as the model for the sage in The Razor's Edge (1944). Arthur Osborne wrote the standard biography and founded The Mountain Path (1964). A review in that journal sent Ramesh Balsekar to Nisargadatta. David Godman, the ashram's historian, wrote the books that record most of these lives.

The ashram

Sri Ramanasramam is run by his brother's family: Niranjanānanda Swāmi (until 1953), T. N. Venkataraman (1953–94), V. S. Ramanan (1994–2020), and today Dr. Venkat S. Ramanan.

Nāth · Inchegiri

Matsyendranāth → Gorakhnāth ⇢ Inchegiri → Siddharameshwar → Nisargadatta

The Nāth yogis are a separate Śaiva tradition, not Śaṅkara's teachers, and several centuries later. The householder Inchegiri line claims descent from them, and through it came Nisargadatta Maharaj. His talks were recorded as I Am That by Maurice Frydman, a devotee of Ramana.

Where the branches meet

The branches share roots but never merged. They touch through a handful of documented meetings and several parallels, all listed under Between the lineages on the tree page.
