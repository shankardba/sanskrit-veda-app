Tamil / Characters

Tamil is Dravidian, not Indo-Aryan — unlike Hindi or Marathi, it isn't descended from Sanskrit, and its script isn't Devanagari's sibling either. Modern Tamil script was assembled in the 7th century in the Pallava kingdom, simplifying Pallava-Grantha letters and folding in some from the older vaṭṭeḻuttu ("round script"), itself a separate 5th–6th century offshoot of Tamil-Brahmi — which, like Devanagari, ultimately traces back to the original Brahmi script, just down a very different branch. The letterforms were simplified again for print in the 19th century and standardized further in 20th-century reforms that removed most of the script's old irregular ligatures.

Consonants — மெய் எழுத்து (mey eḻuttu, "body letters")

Twelve of the eighteen line up into the same kind of place-of-articulation grid Sanskrit uses — one row of stops (வல்லினம், vallinam, "hard"), one row of the matching nasal (மெல்லினம், mellinam, "soft") — but only two rows, not five: Tamil's stops carry no voiced/aspirated counterparts of their own. க், for instance, is just /k/ — the same letter surfaces as [g] or [x] depending on where it falls in a word, rather than being written with a different letter the way Sanskrit's க/ग/ख/घ distinction would require.

The special letter — ஃ (āyta eḻuttu)

ஃ
ḵ

Classified in Tamil grammar as neither vowel nor consonant — a leftover of an old Dravidian sound lost almost everywhere else, kept today mainly to spell foreign sounds Tamil's own eighteen consonants can't reach (ஃப for English f, as in coffee — காஃபி).

Letters for Sanskrit & other loanwords — கிரந்த எழுத்து (grantha)

Six more letters, borrowed from the Grantha script Sanskrit itself was once written in across the Tamil country, cover sounds Tamil's native eighteen don't distinguish — most visibly, the difference between Tamil's single plain ஸ/ச-type sounds and Sanskrit's separate ś/ṣ/s/h. Tamil almost never fuses two consonants into a single conjunct glyph the way Devanagari does — க்ஷ (kṣa) and the ligature ஶ்ரீ (śrī) are the standard exceptions. Vel Maaral's own colophon spells the same word ஸ்ரீ instead, with ஸ் (plain s) rather than ஶ் (ś) — both spellings are attested for it in ordinary use, since spoken Tamil doesn't distinguish the two sounds either way.

Building a syllable

Every consonant is written with its vowel already suppressed (a small mark, புள்ளி/puḷḷi, does this — the dot under க் above). Combine it with one of the twelve vowels and the puḷḷi is replaced by that vowel's own attached form, giving a new glyph for the syllable — the same "consonant + vowel = one shape" idea as Sanskrit's mātrā signs, just carried out for the full vowel set rather than a subset of them.

க் (k) run through all twelve vowels. Every one of Tamil's eighteen consonants generates the same twelve forms, for 18 × 12 = 216 combination letters — plus the 12 vowels, 18 bare consonants, and ஃ, for 247 characters traditionally counted as "the Tamil alphabet."
