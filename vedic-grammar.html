Sanskrit / Vedic Grammar

Vedic Sanskrit is the language of the Ṛgveda and the other three Vedas — composed and transmitted orally roughly 1500–1000 BCE, centuries before writing reached the language. It's also, specifically, the language behind this site's Sanskrit chants: the Chamakam and Namakam are Krishna Yajurveda, the Soundarya Lahari a later Sanskrit hymn closer to the classical end of the spectrum. As Vedic speech evolved, a grammatical tradition arose specifically to preserve the hymns against corruption, culminating in Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī (~4th century BCE) — the point usually treated as where "Vedic" ends and "Classical" begins. This page assumes the Grammar page and covers only what's actually different in the older language, rather than re-describing the whole system.

Declension: root nouns were the norm

Classical Sanskrit is dominated by thematic (a-stem) nouns like देव (deva) — endings attached after an inserted "theme vowel." Vedic leans heavily on the older, inherited athematic pattern instead: endings suffixed directly onto a consonant-final root, no theme vowel, with heavier sandhi at the seam. पाद् (pā́d-, "foot") declines pā́t (nom.), pad-ā́ (instr.), pad-é (dat.) — compare how much more exposed the bare root is here than in devena, devāya. Classical Sanskrit still keeps a residue of root nouns, but thematic stems became the overwhelming default.

A finer feminine ī-stem distinction

Vedic distinguishes two classes of feminine ī-stem noun that Classical Sanskrit merges into one: the vṛkī́s-type (accent staying on the -ī, nominative singular keeping its -s) and the devī́-type. The difference is purely about which of two inherited accent/ending patterns a given ī-stem word follows — by the Classical period, one pattern absorbed the other.

Three past tenses that actually meant different things

Classical Sanskrit keeps the imperfect, perfect, and aorist as grammatical categories, but their meanings have collapsed together into one general past. In Vedic they were semantically distinct: the imperfect was the general narrative past, the perfect worked as a present-perfect ("has done") and sometimes a plain present, and the aorist marked a proximal present-perfect ("just now did"). The perfect system also built distinct strong and weak stem forms (singular active vs. everything else) and, beyond the indicative, its own subjunctive, optative, and imperative — all lost in Classical, which forms only perfect indicatives.

A fifth mood

Alongside indicative, optative, and imperative, Vedic had a living subjunctive/injunctive mood — the injunctive survives narrowly into Classical only in prohibitions with mā (mā bhūḥ, "don't be"). The "conditional" mood counted on the Grammar page's four-mood Classical list is itself a later, separate development.

A dozen infinitives, not one

Classical Sanskrit has exactly one infinitive form, built from the accusative case. Vedic could form infinitives from at least four different cases — dative, accusative, ablative-genitive, and locative — with more than a dozen suffix patterns attested overall, most of them rare. Classical inherited only the accusative type and dropped the rest.

Accent was still alive

The udātta/anudātta/svarita pitch-accent marks already catalogued on the Characters page, and visible throughout this site's chant text, were a fully phonemic part of Vedic — accent placement is exactly what decided whether a root took guṇa, vṛddhi, or zero grade (see Grammar). That system was still active in Pāṇini's own time, but had faded from common speech within a few centuries of the Classical standard being fixed.

Shorter compounds, looser sandhi

The same four compound types on the Grammar page already existed in Vedic, but far more modestly used — the Ṛgveda and Atharvaveda have no compounds longer than three members, and even three-member compounds are rare (pūrva-kāma-kṛ́tvan, "fulfilling former wishes," is one of the longest). Classical Sanskrit's later literature would go on to chain a dozen or more members into a single compound. External sandhi was also more variable in Vedic than the standardized rules Classical settled on.
