The machine

Every sound law is a rule of the form "this sound becomes that sound, in this setting". RUKI, for example, is s → ṣ after r, u, k or i. Run one branch's rules over a reconstructed ancestor word, in historical order, and out comes that branch's word. Sanskrit and Slavic start from the same word. They differ only in which rules they ran, and when. The coloured tags are the same patterns as the Cognate Explorer.

Why order matters

A rulebook is a list, not a set. Take "in the wolf", PIE *wĺ̥kʷoy. Both branches soften k before a front vowel, and both later shrink the diphthong oy into a front vowel (Sanskrit e, Slavic ě). If the softening came first, the k was still in front of an o and survives: Sanskrit vṛke, Slavic vьlcě. Run the same rules in the other order and the k softens by mistake: vṛce, vьlčě. Neither is real. This is how linguists work out the relative order of changes nobody wrote down.

Slavic even shows both orders at once. Its second palatalization came after the diphthongs shrank, so it does soften that k, but to c, not č, which is why the Old Church Slavonic form is vьlcě.

Where the machine breaks

Speakers level their paradigms

predicted vṛca · attested vṛka "O wolf!"
The Law of Palatals should turn the vocative's k into c. Sanskrit says vṛka anyway, because every other form of the word has k and speakers restored it. Sound laws are regular, but analogy reaches in afterwards.

Not every law is settled

*h₂ówis → predicted āvi- · attested avi- "sheep"
Brugmann's law is what gives vāhayati its long ā, but words like "sheep" don't follow it. Linguists still argue about its exact conditions. Type h₂ówis into "Your own" to watch it misfire.

A simplified rulebook

The real histories have dozens more steps: accent, sandhi between words, later vowel changes. The machine stops at Sanskrit and Proto-Slavic. The Russian and Serbian/Croatian forms are shown as they are today, not derived. Your own words come out as predictions, not facts.

The same idea as an L-system

An L-system grows a plant by rewriting a string with a fixed rule list. Sound change is the same kind of machine with two differences. Each rule runs once per historical stage instead of over and over, and the rules look at neighbouring sounds: RUKI fires only after r, u, k or i.

Every preset word derives to its attested Sanskrit and Proto-Slavic form under these rules, except the vocative noted above. Reconstructions follow Wiktionary's Proto-Slavic entries (*buditi, *voziti, *vьlkъ) and standard handbooks. Slavic forms are Proto-Slavic in the usual scholarly spelling, with ъ and ь for the short "jer" vowels and y for the vowel of Russian ы.
