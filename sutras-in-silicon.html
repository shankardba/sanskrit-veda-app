Sūtras in Silicon

Mental shortcuts from Vedic Mathematics that turn up again inside computers — and where they stop.

Mathematics / Sūtras in Silicon

The methods in Bhāratī Kṛṣṇa Tīrtha’s book were designed for a person working in base 10. A computer works in base 2⁶⁴ and multiplies two 64-bit numbers in a single instruction, so for ordinary-sized numbers no digit-by-digit trick can be faster. But for very large numbers — the hundreds of digits used in cryptography and exact arithmetic — several of the same ideas are standard techniques, discovered independently and known by other names.

Method by method

Each Vedic name links to its animated lesson.

in use

Vertically and crosswise

Ūrdhva-Tiryagbhyām → Comba (product-scanning) multiplication
Computing the answer one column at a time — all the products that land in the same place, then the carry — is how big-number libraries multiply small and medium operands. P. G. Comba (1990) showed that column order needs far fewer memory writes than row-by-row multiplication.
in use

Duplex squaring

Dvandva-yoga → symmetric squaring
When both factors are the same, every crosswise product appears twice. Libraries such as GMP compute each once and double it — the duplex — which makes squaring markedly cheaper than general multiplication.
in use

All from nine, the last from ten

Nikhilam division → pseudo-Mersenne reduction
Dividing by a number just below a power of the base reduces to multiplying by its small complement and adding. Cryptographers choose primes for exactly this: Curve25519 works modulo 2²⁵⁵ − 19, so every reduction is a Nikhilam step with complement 19 — and it runs in every modern secure web connection.
in use

The vinculum

bar digits → Booth recoding · signed-digit arithmetic
Writing 576 as 6 2̄ 4̄ trades big digits for small negative ones. In binary the same idea — digits −1, 0, 1 — cuts the number of additions in hardware multipliers (A. D. Booth, 1951) and in elliptic-curve arithmetic (the non-adjacent form); redundant signed digits also let fast dividers add without waiting for carries (A. Avizienis, 1961).
in use

Osculation

Veṣṭana → Montgomery reduction
Each osculation step adds a multiple of the divisor that makes the last digit 0, then drops that digit. That is precisely one step of P. L. Montgomery’s reduction (1985), the workhorse of RSA and elliptic-curve arithmetic — done in base 2⁶⁴ instead of base 10.
in use

Recurring decimals by the ekādhika

Ekādhikena Pūrveṇa → multiply-with-carry random numbers
Generating 1/19 from the right — multiply by 2, keep the carry — is G. Marsaglia’s multiply-with-carry generator. With multiplier a and base b it outputs the digits of a fraction with denominator ab − 1; with a = 2 and b = 10 that fraction is 1/19.
same method

Straight division

Dhvajāṅka → Knuth’s Algorithm D
Guessing each quotient digit from the leading digit(s) of the divisor and then correcting by the rest is the standard long-division algorithm for big numbers (Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, vol. 2). Same method, no speed-up.
same method

Square and cube roots digit by digit

Vargamūla → digit-recurrence roots
Root extraction one digit at a time, subtracting duplexes, is the digit-recurrence method used in some hardware square-root units. Software usually prefers Newton’s iteration, which doubles the number of correct digits each step.
in use

Casting out nines

Guṇita-samuccaya → checksums and residue checks
Checking a calculation by its remainder modulo 9 is a residue check. Some processors check their arithmetic units with small residues, and the Internet checksum is arithmetic modulo 2¹⁶ − 1 — casting out in base 2¹⁶.
no gain

Special-case tricks

squares ending in 5 · × 999 · Antyayor → none
These depend on base-10 patterns that vanish in binary, and on numbers small enough to fit one machine word — where a single hardware multiply takes a few clock cycles. No gain.

The ceiling

Vertically-and-crosswise multiplication takes about n² digit products for n-digit numbers. A. A. Karatsuba (1962) needed only about n1.58, Toom–Cook and FFT-based methods fewer still, and D. Harvey and J. van der Hoeven (2019) reached n log n. Libraries therefore use the column method only below a few dozen machine words and switch to these for anything larger. None of the sūtras beats them.

Where something may remain

Decimal arithmetic. Financial software, decimal floating point and big numbers stored in base 10⁹ really do work in powers of ten, so Nikhilam-style reduction by divisors near 10ᵏ and Ekādhika-style generation of recurring decimals apply directly. Explanation. The sūtras give short, memorable descriptions of algorithms that textbooks often present only as code — a useful way to teach why they work.
