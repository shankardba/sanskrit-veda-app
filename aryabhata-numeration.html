Āryabhaṭa Numeration

How a 6th-century astronomer packed numbers into verse, one Sanskrit syllable at a time.

Mathematics / Āryabhaṭa Numeration

Āryabhaṭa (b. 476 CE) opens his Āryabhaṭīya — the foundational Sanskrit astronomy and mathematics text, written around 499 CE — with a chapter called the Gītikāpada, "the verse of song." Its second stanza doesn't teach astronomy yet; it teaches a code. Sanskrit consonants and vowels, combined the same way they combine into any ordinary syllable, stand in for digits and decimal places — so an entire number, however large, can be spelled as a pronounceable word and dropped straight into a metrical verse. Āryabhaṭa used it to encode the astronomical constants throughout the rest of the book, from small counts up into the billions.

The core rule

Every syllable is a consonant plus a vowel — and each half does a different job:

Consonant → the digit (1–100)

33 consonants, valued 1 through 100
The 25 "class" (varga) consonants — the same five-by-five stop grid from the Characters page — count 1 to 25 in reading order.
The 8 "non-class" (avarga) consonants — य र ल व श ष स ह — count by tens, 30 to 100.

Vowel → the place (a power of 100)

9 vowels, each 100× the last
अ इ उ ऋ ऌ ए ऐ ओ औ step through 100⁰, 100¹, 100²… up to 100⁸ — so each vowel shifts a value two decimal digits higher than the one before it, the same nine vowels already catalogued on the Characters page.

Put together: value(consonant + vowel) = consonant's number × 100(vowel's position, 0–8). ख (kha=2) alone is 2; खि (khi) — kha with the second vowel, i — is 2 × 100¹ = 200; खु (khu) is 2 × 100² = 20,000, and so on through खौ (khau) = 2 × 10¹⁶.

The Āryabhaṭa system doesn't distinguish long from short vowels — आ counts as अ, ई as इ, and so on — so only the short forms are needed here. (Sanskrit has no separate long ऌ̄ in ordinary use, which is why this ladder stops needing that distinction at all.)

Building a two-digit value

A single place (one vowel) can hold any value from 0 to 99 — bigger than the 25-cap of a lone varga consonant — because more than one consonant can share the same vowel, with their values simply added together. ग (3) alone covers "3"; but "58" needs two syllables at once: ल (50, avarga) plus ज (8, varga), both spoken with the vowel अ — ja-la. Since a value like 24 sits inside a single varga consonant's own 1–25 range, it could be written as one syllable (भ, bha=24) or split into two summed ones (घ 4 + न 20) — both are valid, and which one a real verse uses often comes down to the meter, not a fixed rule.

Reading order

The one habit to unlearn: Āryabhaṭa writes the smallest place first. A number is spelled ones-pair, then hundreds-pair, then myriads-pair — ascending, left to right — the reverse of the modern convention of writing the largest digit first. Reading any example on this page as ordinary left-to-right place value will get the number backwards.

Strung together in that same ascending order: जल घिनि झुशु झृसृ खॢ (ja-la ghi-ni jhu-śu jhṛ-sṛ khḷ) — a five-syllable word encoding a nine-digit number.

A genuine example survives too: ङिशिबुणॢष्खृ (ṅiśibuṇḷṣkhṛ) encodes 1,582,237,500 in a real historical source, though its 100⁶ and 100⁸ syllables are swapped and merged into a ligature for reasons that aren't fully understood. The Āryabhaṭīya's own verse for a table of sines is a genuine, if far more intricate, use of the system by Āryabhaṭa himself. The system may also have been able to express fractions and mixed fractions in specific contexts — Wikipedia's own account of this is incomplete, so it isn't reproduced here as settled.

Two related but distinct Sanskrit numeral-encoding systems exist: the kaṭapayādi system (a later cipher mapping consonants to digits 0–9 for cyclic, calendar, and mnemonic use) and the bhūtasaṃkhya system (numbers expressed as ordinary words for things conventionally associated with that count, like "eyes" for two). Neither is Āryabhaṭa's alphasyllabic system, and both are candidates for future pages here.
